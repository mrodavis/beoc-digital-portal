Researching & Citing Sources

Module 8: Microsoft 365 and More

Find and judge sources for a document, then record them with Word's References tools: citation style, Insert Citation, Source Manager, and a bibliography. Use the Thesaurus to choose words carefully.

By the end of this lesson you can:
- Judge whether a source is reliable enough to use before you cite it
- Set a citation style and insert citations with References > Insert Citation
- Reuse sources from earlier documents with Manage Sources (Source Manager)
- Insert a bibliography and update it after you add sources
- Replace a word with a synonym using the Thesaurus


## Finding sources is your job; formatting them is Word's
 type: scenario
 role: You are drafting a patient education handout at Lakeside Medical Associates.
 text: The handout says that a reading of 130/80 or higher is considered high blood pressure for most adults, and that home readings can be more reliable than a single office reading. Your supervisor asks where each claim came from. A browser search returns dozens of pages, and they range from a medical journal to a blog selling supplements.
 task: Choose sources you can defend, then cite them so every claim in the handout can be traced.

Research and citation are two separate jobs. Finding a source and deciding whether to trust it happens in your web browser and in your own judgment. Recording the source and formatting the citation happens in Word, on the References tab, where Word builds each in-text citation and the bibliography in the style you choose.

Note: Older guides show two research panes inside Word: Smart Lookup (right-click a word > Search) and Researcher (References > Researcher). Microsoft retired both. Smart Lookup was retired starting January 1, 2025, and Researcher was removed from Word for Windows and Mac between late December 2024 and January 2025. If an older computer still shows them, do not rely on them. Search for sources in a web browser instead, and cite them with the References tab as this lesson shows.

 type: key-terms
 terms:
   term: Citation
   definition: A short reference in the text, such as (Alvarez, 2024), that points the reader to a full entry in the bibliography.
   term: Bibliography
   definition: The list of every source cited, at the end of the document. APA calls it References; MLA calls it Works Cited.
   term: Citation style
   definition: A set of formatting rules (APA, MLA, Chicago and others). Use the style your instructor or employer asks for.
   term: Source Manager
   definition: The Manage Sources dialog. Its Master List holds sources from your earlier documents; its Current List holds the sources in this document.


## Judging a source before you use it
A citation makes a claim traceable; it does not make it correct. Before a source goes into a document, ask four questions about it. A source that fails any of them needs a second look, and for health information that patients will rely on, the bar is high.

 type: table
 caption: Four questions for any source
 columns:
   Question
   Look for
   Warning signs
 rows:
    Who published it?
    A named author or organization with relevant expertise: a medical school, a government health agency, a peer-reviewed journal
    No author or organization, or a seller of the product being discussed
    When?
    A publication or review date recent enough for the topic
    No date, or guidance old enough to have been replaced
    What is the evidence?
    References to studies or data, and claims that match other reliable sources
    Promises of a cure, "one simple trick," or claims nobody else makes
    Why does it exist?
    To inform or educate
    To sell, persuade, or collect your information

 type: worked-example
 title: Choosing between three search results
 task: Find a source for the sentence about home blood pressure readings in the handout.
 steps:
   move: Open each result and find the publisher and the date before you read the content.
   why: Who wrote something and when tells you how much weight it can carry. Reading first and checking later makes it easy to talk yourself into a source because it says what you hoped.
   move: Keep the journal article: a named author, a 2024 review in a health journal, with references to studies.
   why: It passes all four questions. A review article summarizes many studies, which is exactly the kind of support a general claim needs.
   move: Skip the blog post promising to cure high blood pressure in seven days.
   why: It fails on evidence and purpose: it sells a product and makes a claim no clinical source supports. Citing it would make the whole handout look careless.
   move: Write down what Word will need: author, title, year, and the website or journal name.
   why: These are the fields of Word's Create Source dialog. Capturing them while the page is open saves a second search later.
 result: One defensible source per claim, with the details Word needs already in hand.
 takeaway: Check who and when before what. Then have clinical staff review any claim about care before the handout goes to patients.

Note: If your school or employer's Microsoft 365 account includes Copilot, a Copilot button appears in the corner of your Word document. Copilot in Word can summarize a long document you have open or answer questions about it, which can speed up reading a source. It works within Word and your Microsoft 365 content, so it does not replace searching for sources, and its answers still need checking: confirm every fact in the original source and cite that source. If you do not see Copilot, your account does not include it or your organization has not turned it on. Never paste patient information into an AI tool your employer has not approved for it.


## Choosing a citation style
Choose the style before you insert citations so they all come out in the same format. On the References tab, in the Citations & Bibliography group, select the arrow next to Style and pick the style you need, such as APA or MLA.


## Inserting a citation
1. Click at the end of the sentence or phrase you want to cite.
2. On the References tab, select Insert Citation > Add New Source.
3. In the Create Source dialog, choose the Type of Source (for example, Web site or Journal Article) and fill in the author, title, year and the other fields. Select Show All Bibliography Fields if you need more.
4. Select OK. Word inserts the citation at the cursor and saves the source in the document.

To cite a source that is already in the document, click where the citation goes, select Insert Citation, and choose the source from the list at the top of the menu. If you do not have a source's details yet, choose Add New Placeholder and fill it in later.


## Managing sources
Select References > Manage Sources to open Source Manager. The Master List holds sources you created in earlier documents, and the Current List holds the sources in this document. Select a source in the Master List and choose Copy to bring it into this document; it then appears under Insert Citation. Use Edit to correct a source's details and Delete to remove one you no longer need.

The Master List remembers every source you have created, including weak ones. Reuse a source only after asking the same four questions you would ask about a new one.


## Adding the bibliography
Click where the list of sources belongs, usually on its own page at the end. Select References > Bibliography and choose a built-in format, such as References for APA or Works Cited for MLA. Word lists every source in the document in the current style.

The bibliography does not change by itself. After you cite a new source, click anywhere in the bibliography and select Update Citations and Bibliography.


## Using the Thesaurus
To vary a word you have used too often, right-click it and choose Synonyms for a quick list, or click the word and select Review > Thesaurus to open the Thesaurus pane. Point to a result, select the arrow, and choose Insert to replace your word.

Note: Two cautions. In health writing a synonym can change the meaning: "chronic" and "long-lasting" are close, but "hypertension" and "stress" are not, so check each replacement. And swapping a source's words for synonyms is not paraphrasing. Quote a source's exact words in quotation marks, or restate the idea fully in your own words, and cite it either way.


## Knowledge Check
Check yourself: Smart Lookup and Researcher are gone from Word. Where do you find sources for a document now? Answer: In a web browser, judging each source before you use it. Microsoft retired Smart Lookup and Researcher in January 2025. You search in a browser and decide which sources are reliable; Word's References tab then records and formats the citations.

Check yourself: You cite a new source after inserting the bibliography. What do you do so the new source appears in it? Answer: Click in the bibliography and select Update Citations and Bibliography. A bibliography is built from the sources in the document when you insert it. After you add a source, update it so the list matches what you cite.
