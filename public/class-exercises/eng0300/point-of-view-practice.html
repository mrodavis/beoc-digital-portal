PART 1 10 minutes ✓ Complete

Warm-Up: Who Is Talking?

Read each sentence. Who is speaking, and where are they? Tap the point of view.

Notebook first: number 1 to 6 and write the point of view for each sentence.
First person = the speaker is part of the events (I, me, my, we, us).
Second person = the speaker talks to the reader (you, your). This is common in directions.
Third person = the speaker is outside the events (he, she, they, names).

1. “I grabbed my badge and ran for the 7:10 bus.”

First person. "I" and "my" show the speaker is inside the story.

2. “Before you start the machine, put on your safety glasses.”

Second person. The speaker talks to "you." Directions often use second person.

3. “Marcus checked the schedule twice before he called his manager.”

Third person. The speaker tells about Marcus from the outside: "Marcus," "he," "his."

4. “We waited forty minutes, and nobody told us why.”

First person. "We" and "us" put the speaker in the group that waited.

5. “The nurses changed shifts at 7:00, and the new team read each chart.”

Third person. No "I" or "you." The speaker describes other people.

6. “You will need two forms of ID when you arrive.”

Second person. "You" speaks straight to the reader.

Guided Example: One Event, Two Points of View

Both texts are about the same broken elevator. As you read, fill in the chart in your notebook.

TEXT A: Six Flights, AgainFor the third time this month, I carried my groceries up six flights of stairs. My knees ached by the fourth floor. Mrs. Lopez from 5C sat down on the landing because she could not go on, so I carried her bags too. I called the management office and got a recording. Honestly, I feel like nobody in that office has ever climbed these stairs.
TEXT B: Notice to ResidentsThe elevator at 418 Fulton Street stopped working on Tuesday because a motor part failed. A repair company inspected the elevator on Wednesday and ordered a new part. The part is expected to arrive within five business days. Residents who need help with deliveries may call the front desk at extension 2.
Notebook chart: two columns, Text A and Text B. Four rows: Who is speaking, Point of view, What the text focuses on, and Feelings shown?

1. Who is telling Text A?

"I carried my groceries up six flights" shows the speaker lives there and lived through the problem.

2. Text B is told in:

Text B has no "I" or "we." It reports facts about the elevator from the outside.

3. Which detail could only come from a first-person speaker?

Only someone inside the events can tell us how their own body felt.

4. How does point of view change what each text focuses on?

First person brings feelings and personal detail. Third person here gives causes, dates, and next steps.

PART 4 25 minutes · hand in ✓ Complete

Fact or Opinion?

A fact can be checked and proven true or false. An opinion is what someone thinks, feels, or believes.

Opinion clue words: best, worst, should, too, great, terrible, I think, most important.
Watch out: a sentence can be about opinions and still be a fact. "Most workers in the survey said they wanted more training" is a fact, because you can check what the survey said.

1. “The clinic opens at 8:00 a.m. on weekdays.”

Fact. You can check the hours.

2. “The clinic has the friendliest staff in Brooklyn.”

Opinion. "Friendliest" is a judgment. People can disagree.

3. “The new schedule adds two buses every hour.”

Fact. You can count the buses on the schedule.

4. “The new schedule is a huge improvement.”

Opinion. "Huge improvement" is how the writer feels about it.

5. “Forty-two students signed up for the evening class.”

Fact. You can check the sign-up list.

6. “Evening classes are too hard for working parents.”

Opinion. "Too hard" is a judgment, and it is not true for every parent.

7. “Most workers in the survey said they wanted more training.”

Fact. It reports what the survey said. You can check the survey.

8. “The survey proves that training is the most important benefit.”

Opinion. "Most important" is a judgment. A survey shows what people said; it does not prove what matters most.

9. “The library should stay open until 10:00 p.m.”

Opinion. "Should" tells what the writer wants.

10. “The library's hours were cut from 60 to 48 hours a week.”

Fact. You can check the posted hours.
PART 5 20 minutes · hand in ✓ Complete

Author's Purpose

Why did the author write this? Tap the purpose.

Inform = give facts. Persuade = get you to believe or do something. Entertain = tell a story or make you laugh. Explain = tell how to do something, or why something happens.

1. “Vote YES on Question 2! Our parks need this money, and our kids deserve better.”

Persuade. It tells you what to do and gives reasons to agree.

2. “Step 1: Log in to Brightspace. Step 2: Click Assessments. Step 3: Choose Assignments.”

Explain. It shows you how to do something, step by step.

3. “Last year, the Eastside Library lent out 40,000 books and 3,000 laptops.”

Inform. It gives facts and numbers. It does not ask you to do anything.

4. “The raccoon stared at me through the kitchen window like it owned the place, then calmly ate my entire pizza.”

Entertain. It tells a funny little story.

5. “Your phone battery drains faster in the cold because low temperatures slow down the chemical reactions inside it.”

Explain. It tells why something happens. "Because" is the clue.

6. “Don't wait until December. Sign up for spring classes this week, before the best time slots fill up.”

Persuade. It pushes you to act now.

TABE-Style Set

Read the passage two times. Then answer in your notebook before you tap.

WHY I TAKE THE NIGHT SHIFTPeople always ask me why I work nights at the hospital lab. The honest answer is that nights fit my life. My shift runs from 11:00 p.m. to 7:00 a.m., which lets me walk my son to school every morning before I sleep. The lab also pays two dollars more per hour for the night shift. Of course, night work is not for everyone. Some of my coworkers say they never feel fully rested, and I believe them. Still, I think more working parents should consider it. When you work while your family sleeps, you get to be there for the moments that matter most.

POINT OF VIEW
1. The passage is told from which point of view?

"I," "me," and "my" show the speaker is the night worker.

FACT OR OPINION
2. Which sentence from the passage is a FACT?

Shift hours can be checked. The other three are the writer's beliefs and feelings.

AUTHOR'S PURPOSE
3. What is the author's main purpose?

"I think more working parents should consider it" is the writer's main point. The facts support that opinion.

AUTHOR'S CHOICES
4. Why does the author include what the coworkers say?

Admitting the other side makes a persuasive writer sound honest and fair.
