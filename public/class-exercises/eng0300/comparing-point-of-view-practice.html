Goals

Where this fits: In the Point of View & Purpose practice you worked with one text at a time: who is telling it (the narrator or speaker) and why the author wrote it. Here you put two texts about the same event side by side.

Did you watch the preview video? Tell your partner the three things you compare when two writers describe the same event. Missed it? Watch it here (5 minutes).

By the end of this practice, you will be able to:

  1. Name each writer's point of view and position on the same event.
  2. Chart what each writer includes, leaves out, and emphasizes.
  3. Explain in one sentence how a writer's position shapes what the reader is shown.

Why it matters: Notices from your job, news stories, and ads all choose what to tell you and what to leave out. The TABE also asks you to compare two passages on the same topic.

PART 1 about 10 minutes ✓ Complete

Warm-Up: Spot the Slant

Writers pick words to make you feel a certain way about the same thing. Tap the slant of each phrase.

Notebook first: number 1 to 6 and write Positive, Negative, or Neutral.

1. “a bold new plan”

Positive. "Bold" makes the plan sound brave.

2. “a reckless scheme”

Negative. "Reckless" and "scheme" make it sound dangerous and sneaky.

3. “a new plan”

Neutral. No feeling words. Just the fact.

4. “the crowded, noisy clinic”

Negative. "Crowded" and "noisy" make it sound unpleasant.

5. “the busy, lively clinic”

Positive. The same crowd, but "lively" makes it sound good.

6. “the clinic, which saw 80 patients today”

Neutral. A number you can check, with no feeling words.
Key idea: same facts, different words, different feeling. When you read, ask: which words is the writer using to push me?
Talk it out (1 minute): Think of one slanted word you have seen at work, in the news, or in an ad. Tell your partner the word and whether it pushes the reader in a positive or negative direction.

Guided Example: Two Accounts, One Event

Both texts are about the same change at a community center. As you read, fill in the chart in your notebook.

TEXT A: Notice from the Center DirectorStarting November 1, the Wilson Street Community Center will close at 7:00 p.m. instead of 10:00 p.m. The change will save about $40,000 a year in staff and energy costs. Evening visits have dropped by nearly a third over the past two years, and shorter weeknight hours will let us keep all weekend programs open. We thank our members for their understanding.
TEXT B: A Letter from a ParentFor two years, my daughter has done her homework at the Wilson Street center every night after her shift at the grocery store. It is the only quiet, safe place she can go after 7:00. Now the center will close its doors at the exact time she arrives. Nobody asked the families who actually use the evening hours. A "savings" of a few dollars will cost our kids much more.
Talk it out (1 minute): Who wrote each text, and what does each writer want the reader to think? Use the frame: "Text A is written by ________, who wants readers to ________."
Notebook chart: two columns, Text A and Text B. Four rows: Includes, Leaves out, Emphasizes, and Slanted words.
Commit first: For questions 1 to 5, write your answer (A, B, C, or D) in your notebook first. Then tap to check.

1. Who is speaking in Text A?

"We thank our members" shows the writer speaks for the center.

2. What does Text A include that Text B leaves out?

Both texts give the closing time and the name. Only Text A gives the cost savings and the drop in visits.

3. What does Text B include that Text A leaves out?

Text A never mentions the people who use the evening hours. Text B is built around one of them.

4. Text B puts the word "savings" in quotation marks. This shows the writer:

Quotation marks around one word can show doubt: "so-called savings."

5. Which statement best compares the two points of view?

Each writer's position decides what goes in, what stays out, and which words are used.
Text AText B
IncludesCost savings, fewer evening visits, weekend programsOne student's routine, safety, no one asked families
Leaves outWho uses the evening hours and where they will goWhy the center made the change and the numbers
EmphasizesMoney and planningPeople and loss

Pair check (2 minutes): Compare your notebook chart with your partner's and with the finished chart. Add one thing you missed, and be ready to say what you added.
PART 3 about 15 minutes · on your own, then pair check · hand in ✓ Complete

Practice Pair: TABE-Style

Read both texts. Then answer in your notebook before you tap.

TEXT A: Fresh Market Adds Self-CheckoutFresh Market is proud to open six new self-checkout lanes at its Atlantic Avenue store this month. The modern lanes let shoppers with a few items pay in under a minute. During the first week, the average wait at the front of the store fell from nine minutes to four. Self-checkout is the future of shopping, and our customers deserve the fastest trip possible.
TEXT B: What the Machines Don't ScanI have worked register 3 at Fresh Market for six years. When the self-checkout machines arrived, my hours went from 32 a week to 24. The machines are fast when everything works, but I spend half my shift walking over to fix them when they beep "unexpected item." Last Tuesday, an older man asked me to help him because he could not read the small screen. Nobody asked the cashiers what customers really need.

1. Text B is told from the point of view of:

"I have worked register 3" shows the speaker is a cashier, in first person.

2. Which information does Text A leave out?

Text A tells only the good news. Text B tells us the cashier's hours dropped from 32 to 24.

3. Which sentence from Text A is an OPINION?

No one can prove what "the future of shopping" is. The others can be checked.

4. How is the word choice in the two texts different?

Text A uses "proud" and "modern." Text B uses "beep" and "nobody asked."
Pair check (2 minutes): Compare answers with your partner. If you disagree, find the sentence in the text that settles it and read it out loud.
Done early? Rewrite one sentence from Text A so it sounds like the cashier wrote it. Keep the same facts.
PART 4 about 20 minutes · hand in ✓ Complete

Includes, Leaves Out, Emphasizes

Here are eight facts about one change. Two writers will use them in different ways.

FACT BANK: ROUTE 9 BUS CHANGE
  1. Starting November 2, the Route 9 bus will make 20 stops instead of 32.
  2. The shorter route will save the transit agency $1.1 million a year.
  3. Trips on the shorter route will be about 12 minutes faster.
  4. Two of the removed stops are next to a senior center.
  5. Riders at the removed stops will walk up to 10 extra minutes to the nearest stop.
  6. The agency held two public meetings about the change in August.
  7. Only 45 people came to the public meetings.
  8. Route 9 carries about 6,000 riders a day.

1. The transit agency is writing an announcement. Which fact will it most likely put FIRST?

A writer leads with the fact that helps their side most. Faster trips make the change sound good.

2. The director of the senior center writes a letter against the change. What will she most likely emphasize?

She will focus on the facts that show harm to the people she serves.

3. The agency writes, "We held two public meetings in August." Which related fact might it leave out?

"Two public meetings" sounds good. "Only 45 people came" weakens it, so the agency may leave it out.

4. Why is leaving out a fact a way to persuade?

What a writer leaves out can push you as much as what they put in. Always ask: what is missing?
Play the writers (3 minutes): Partner A is the transit agency. Partner B is the senior center director. Each of you says one opening sentence out loud, using facts from the bank. Then switch roles. This is practice for your Audience Switch in Part 5.

PART 5 about 40 minutes ✓ Complete

WRITE IT! Start Your Audience Switch

The writing task for this skill is the Audience Switch: the same facts, written twice, for two different readers. Start it now and finish it when your draft is ready.

Pick your facts: use the Route 9 Fact Bank (Part 4), the community center facts (Part 2), or a set of facts from the Point of View Skill Lab passage.

Version 1: inform a classmate (3 to 4 sentences). Your reader wants to understand, not to be convinced.
  1. Starting ______, ______ will change.
  2. This means ______.
  3. One important fact is ______.
  4. If you ______, you should know ______.
Version 2: persuade a supervisor (3 to 4 sentences). Your reader has to decide something and is short on time.
  1. I recommend that we ______.
  2. The main reason is ______.
  3. This will cost / save ______.
  4. Please ______ by ______.
Last line: In Version 2, I changed ______ because a supervisor ______.
Partner check (3 minutes): Trade drafts. Your partner reads both versions and tells you one word or phrase that changed the tone. Write it down, then keep revising.

Self-check. Tap each line when your draft does it:

1Both versions use the same facts. Version 2 adds no new information.
2The two versions open differently, because the readers need different first sentences.
3You can see a clear change in word choice and tone.
4Version 2 leads with the decision and the cost, not just more enthusiasm.
5I ended with one sentence that names what I changed and why.

When you finish, submit it to your instructor or course folder (BEOC students: the Audience Switch writing folder in Brightspace).

PART 6 about 15 minutes · hand in ✓ Complete

Point of View Review Sprint

One short letter, five questions: everything from this skill. Answer in your notebook, then tap.

Short on time? Stop here and come back to Part 6 later. Your notebook keeps your answers.
LETTER TO THE EDITORTo the editor: The city's plan to plant 500 new trees on Fulton Street is the best news our neighborhood has had in years. The trees will be planted between March and May, and the city will pay for watering them for the first two years. Shade from trees keeps sidewalks cooler on hot days. Some store owners worry that the roots will crack the sidewalks, but that is a small price for cleaner air and cooler summers. Every neighbor should come out to help on planting day, April 12. (Rosa Diaz, Bedford resident)

POINT OF VIEW
1. From which point of view is the letter written?

"Our neighborhood" shows the writer lives there and is part of what she describes.

FACT OR OPINION
2. Which sentence is a FACT?

Planting dates can be checked. The others are the writer's judgments.

AUTHOR'S PURPOSE
3. What is the writer's main purpose?

"Every neighbor should come out to help" is a call to action.

WORD CHOICE
4. The writer says the cracked sidewalks are "a small price." This shows she:

She names the worry, then makes it sound small next to the benefits.

COMPARING POINTS OF VIEW
5. Which other point of view does the writer mention, then quickly push aside?

Naming the other side and answering it makes her argument sound fair. A store owner's letter would tell a different story.

Sum It Up and Exit Ticket

First sum up the lesson. Then answer three quick questions in your notebook before you tap.

Sum It Up (write first, 3 minutes): Finish these two sentences in your notebook. They are part of your hand-in.
  1. Two writers can describe the same event very differently because ________.
  2. Next time I read a notice, an article, or an ad, I will ask ________.
Share out: Read sentence 1 to a partner, or out loud to yourself. Then answer the three exit ticket questions on your own.

1. Two writers describe the same event very differently. The main reason is usually that:

Position and purpose decide what a writer includes, leaves out, and emphasizes.

2. "Reckless," "disaster," and "waste" are words that give a:

These words push you to feel badly about the subject.

3. A writer leaves out a fact that hurts their argument. As a reader, you should:

Comparing two texts on one topic is the best way to see what each one left out.
