Block A — Vocabulary in Context (35 minutes) · Classwork 4, Parts 1–2

Passage I — The Shift Handoff

At Bayview Clinic the change of shift used to take twenty-five minutes. The nurse going home read the whole chart out loud, front to back, while the nurse coming on wrote as fast as she could. Nobody called it a problem. It was simply how the handoff had always been done.

In 2024 the unit tried a printed form with four boxes: what changed, what is pending, what to watch, and who to call. Nurses were told to be concise and to omit anything already written in the chart. The form fit on half a page.

The first month was uncomfortable. Two nurses left out a pending lab result, and the next shift found the discrepancy only when the doctor asked for it. The unit did not throw out the form. It added one line at the bottom: pending results must be listed even if they appear somewhere else.

By spring the handoff took nine minutes and the number of missed follow-ups had dropped by half. The new protocol had not made the nurses faster. It had made them decide, before they spoke, which facts the next shift could not do without.

Part 1 — Context clues. For each word, give its meaning in the passage and name the clue type you used: definition, example, contrast, or general sense.

  • concise · omit · discrepancy · protocol

Part 2 — Roots and prefixes. Give the meaning of each part, then name one other word that uses it.

  • dis– as in discrepancy · pro– as in protocol · –mit as in omit and submit · –cise as in concise and precise

Block B — Multi-Meaning Words and Figurative Language (30 minutes) · Classwork 4, Parts 3–4

Passage J — The Floor Never Sleeps

Marisol had worked the floor at Grand Street Rehab for nine years, and she could read a hallway the way other people read a face. A quiet corridor at ten at night was not the same as a quiet corridor at two in the morning. One meant the unit had settled. The other usually meant somebody was covering for a call-out and the rounds had slipped.

Her supervisor liked to say that the floor never sleeps. Marisol understood it as a warning, not a boast. A unit that ran all night on people working doubles was running on borrowed time.

In March the schedule changed. Two part-time aides became full-time, and the overnight rotation stopped depending on volunteers. Marisol noticed it first in small things: the supply closet stayed stocked, the charting got done before seven, and nobody was sharp with anybody at the end of a shift.

When a new aide asked her what had changed, Marisol said the place had stopped running on fumes. Then, because the aide was new and still learning the building, she said it again in plain words: there were finally enough people to do the work.

Part 3 — Multi-meaning words. Each word below has more than one meaning. Write the meaning that fits this passage, then write one other meaning the word can have.

  • floor · covering · running · sharp

Part 4 — Figurative language. Put each phrase into plain language, in your own words.

  1. "she could read a hallway the way other people read a face"
  2. "the floor never sleeps"
  3. "running on borrowed time"
  4. "stopped running on fumes"

Part 1: Mini-Lesson on the Warm-Up Errors (with me, about 10 minutes)

Each warm-up sentence broke one rule. Here are the five rules.

  1. Two sentences joined by only a comma. A comma cannot hold two complete sentences together. Use a period, a semicolon, or a comma plus and, but, or so.
    Fix: The nurse checked the chart, but she did not sign it.
  2. Neither/nor and either/or. The verb agrees with the subject closest to it.
    Fix: Neither the clerk nor the supervisors were at the desk.
  3. Each, every, and one of. These are singular, so the verb is singular.
    Fix: Each of the patients was given a copy of the form.
  4. Had + the right verb form. After had, use the past participle: had written, had taken, had gone.
    Fix: The manager had written the note before the shift ended.
  5. An opening phrase must describe the word right after the comma. The printer was not walking.
    Fix: Walking into the office, I saw that the printer was already jammed.

Try it. Rewrite each sentence correctly. One error each. Next to each one, write the rule number you used.

  1. The shift ended at seven, the aides stayed to finish the charting.
  2. Either the nurses or the supervisor are signing the form today.
  3. Every one of the rooms were cleaned before noon.
  4. She had took the call before the doctor arrived.
  5. Reading the new schedule, the changes surprised the whole staff.

Part 2: Week 4 Preview: How Is a Text Put Together?

On Monday we move from single words to how a whole text is organized. Writers use a few common patterns, and signal words give each one away.

  • Sequence (steps in order): first, next, then, after that, finally
  • Cause and effect (why something happened and what followed): because, so, as a result, led to
  • Compare and contrast (how two things are alike or different): both, similarly, however, unlike, while
  • Problem and solution (a problem and how it was fixed): the problem, to fix this, one answer, as a result

For each short passage, name the pattern and write down the signal words that told you.

A. Before a patient goes home, the nurse first reviews the medication list. Next, she explains each prescription to the patient and a family member. Finally, the patient signs the discharge form and gets a printed copy.

B. Both the day shift and the night shift use the same handoff form. However, the day shift has more staff on the floor, while the night shift often runs with only two aides. Unlike days, nights leave little time to ask questions.

C. The clinic phone line was busy most mornings, so many patients never got their appointment reminders. As a result, missed visits rose to almost one in five.

D. Patients kept arriving without their insurance cards. To fix this, the front desk began sending a text the day before each visit. One month later, most patients came prepared.

Challenge: Look back at Passage I, The Shift Handoff. Which pattern does the whole passage follow? Give two details that prove it.
