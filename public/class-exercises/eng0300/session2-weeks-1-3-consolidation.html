Block A — Main Ideas & Details (30 minutes) · Classwork 1

Passage D — The Night Shift Library

The Cortland Avenue branch library used to close at six. For a building that sat in the middle of a neighborhood where most adults work until five or later, six o'clock was a door closing just as people arrived.

In 2024 the branch tried something small. On Tuesdays and Thursdays it stayed open until nine, and it did not add a single new program — no classes, no workshops, no author nights. It simply unlocked the doors and kept the lights on.

The librarians expected the late hours to be quiet. Instead, Tuesday and Thursday evenings became the two busiest blocks of the week. Computer stations that sat empty at two in the afternoon had a waiting list by seven. The study rooms filled with high school students whose homes were too crowded to work in. A retired teacher began showing up on her own to help whoever asked.

The branch manager summarized the first year in one sentence at a budget hearing: the neighborhood had never lacked interest in the library, only access to it. The following year, four more branches in the system extended their evening hours.

  1. Which sentence best states the main idea of Passage D?
    A. The Cortland Avenue library used to close at six o'clock.
    B. Low daytime use reflected the branch's hours, not the neighborhood's interest in it.
    C. High school students need quiet places to study.
    D. Libraries should offer more evening programs.
  2. Which detail best supports that main idea?
    A. A retired teacher volunteered her time.
    B. The branch added no new programs, yet evenings became the busiest hours of the week.
    C. Four more branches extended their hours the following year.
    D. The library is in the middle of the neighborhood.
  3. The writer points out twice that the branch added no new programs. Why does that detail matter to the argument? Answer in one or two sentences.
  4. List three details the writer uses to show the evening hours were being used, and say what each one proves.
  5. Summarize Passage D in one sentence of 25 words or fewer, in your own words.
  6. The manager says the neighborhood "had never lacked interest in the library, only access to it." Put that sentence in your own words without using the words interest or access.

Block B — Inferences & Conclusions (30 minutes) · Classwork 2

Passage E — What the Schedule Told Her

Rosa had worked the front desk at the clinic for eleven years, and she could read the appointment book the way a mechanic reads an engine. On Monday morning she noticed something she did not like.

Dr. Okafor's Thursday afternoons had been blocked off for six weeks running. Not filled — blocked. The note in the system said "admin," which the doctors used for paperwork, but Rosa had never known Dr. Okafor to need six straight weeks of it. His patient list for the rest of the week had also grown by four or five names, and two of the newer patients were ones Rosa remembered being assigned to Dr. Weiss.

On Wednesday the practice manager asked Rosa, casually, how long it usually took to train someone on the front desk system. On Friday a woman Rosa did not recognize spent an hour in the back office with the door closed.

Rosa did not ask anyone anything. She updated her resume that weekend. She did not send it out, and she hoped she was wrong, but she had stopped being surprised by things she had seen coming.

  1. The passage never says what Rosa concluded. In one sentence, say what you think she concluded, then list the three details that led you there.
  2. Label each statement STATED or IMPLIED:
    a. Dr. Okafor's Thursday afternoons were blocked for six weeks.
    b. Dr. Weiss is leaving or has already left the practice.
    c. Rosa thinks her own job may be at risk.
    d. A woman Rosa did not recognize was in the back office on Friday.
  3. What single piece of new information would most weaken your conclusion? Explain why in one or two sentences.
  4. Rosa "did not send it out, and she hoped she was wrong." What do those two details tell you about how confident she is? Answer in one or two sentences.
  5. Explain the last line — "she had stopped being surprised by things she had seen coming" — in your own words, and say what it suggests about Rosa's past.

Talk it out (5 minutes)

Tell a partner what you think Rosa concluded. Do you agree with each other? Each of you names the one detail that convinced you most. Then we check Question 2 (STATED or IMPLIED) together as a class.

Block C — Vocabulary in Context (30 minutes) · Classwork 3, Parts 1–4

Passage F — The Words on the Form

When the housing office redesigned its application in 2025, it kept one page almost exactly as it was. That page asked applicants to verify their income, disclose any other assistance they received, and certify that the information was accurate. Staff had assumed for years that the language was clear.

Then the office ran a simple test. It asked forty applicants to explain, in their own words, what each instruction was asking them to do. Twenty-six could not explain disclose. Eleven thought verify meant to write the number down rather than to prove it with a document. Nine read certify as a formality and signed without reading what they were signing.

The office did not lower its standards, and the information it needed did not change. It changed the words. Verify became "show us a document that proves it." Disclose became "tell us about." Certify became "sign here to say this is true."

The share of applications rejected for missing documents fell by more than a third in six months. The office had not made the process easier. It had made the instructions legible, and those are not the same thing.

Part 1 — Context clues. For each word give its meaning in the passage and name the clue type you used: definition, example, contrast, or general sense.

  • verify  ·  disclose  ·  certify  ·  legible

Part 2 — Roots and prefixes. Give the meaning of each part, then name one other word that uses it.

  • dis– as in disclose  ·  –ify as in verify and certify  ·  cert– as in certify and certain

Part 3 — Multi-meaning words. Each word below has more than one common meaning. Write the meaning that applies in the passage, then use a different meaning in a sentence of your own.

  • office  ·  standards  ·  share

Part 4 — The last sentence. The writer says the office "had not made the process easier. It had made the instructions legible, and those are not the same thing." Explain the difference in your own words, in two or three sentences.

Talk it out (5 minutes)

Which word was hardest to figure out? Tell a partner which clue finally helped you. Then share one of your "different meaning" sentences from Part 3 with the class.

Block D — Figurative Language (20 minutes) · Classwork 3, Part 5

Figurative language does not mean exactly what the words say. To find the real meaning, ask two questions: Is this literally true? If not, what is the writer really saying?

Type What it does Example
Simile Compares two things using like or as. The waiting room was as quiet as a library.
Metaphor Says one thing is another thing. No like or as. The front desk was the clinic's control tower.
Idiom A common phrase that means something different from its separate words. She hit the ground running = she started working fast and well from the first day.

Passage G — First Week on the Floor

Dana started at the distribution warehouse on a Monday in March. Her supervisor, Mr. Reyes, warned her that the first week would be a trial by fire, and he was not exaggerating.

The warehouse floor was a beehive. Forklifts beeped, scanners chirped, and workers moved between the aisles like dancers who knew every step. Dana felt like a tourist without a map. By Tuesday her feet ached, and the list of product codes she had to learn seemed a mile long.

On Wednesday a shipment arrived two hours early, and the whole team had to scramble to unload it. Dana was sure she would drop the ball. Instead, an older worker named Pat showed her a trick for reading the shipping labels, and the codes finally started to click. Pat was Dana's lifeline that week.

By Friday Dana was keeping up. She was not the fastest picker on the floor, but she was no longer a fish out of water. When Mr. Reyes asked how the week had gone, she smiled and said she was finally learning the ropes.

Part 5A — Sort it and translate it. Label each expression SIMILE, METAPHOR, or IDIOM. Then write what it really means in plain words.

  1. "The warehouse floor was a beehive."
  2. "Dana felt like a tourist without a map."
  3. "Dana was sure she would drop the ball."
  4. "Pat was Dana's lifeline that week."
  5. "She was finally learning the ropes."

Part 5B — Use the context. Mr. Reyes said the first week would be "a trial by fire." Use the rest of the passage to explain what he meant. Give two details that show he was right.

Part 5C — Literal or figurative? One sentence below is literal and one is figurative. Which is which? What does the figurative one really mean?

  • "By Tuesday her feet ached."
  • "The codes finally started to click."

Part 5D — Write your own. Write one simile and one metaphor about your first day at a job, a school, or a new place. Underline the comparison in each one.

Talk it out (3 minutes)

Read your simile and your metaphor to a partner, but do not explain them. Can your partner tell you what you really meant? If not, what would make it clearer?

Passage H — The Line at Window 3

For years, the financial aid office at a downtown community college had the same problem every September: the line. Students waited an hour or more at Window 3, and many gave up and went home before they reached the front. Staff assumed the office simply needed more workers, but the budget would not allow it.

In 2025 a counselor named Ms. Tran decided to look more closely. For two weeks she wrote down the reason for every visit. The pattern was hard to miss. Nearly half of the students in line had one of the same three questions: where to upload a tax form, how to check whether a form had been received, and what a "verification hold" meant — a term most students misunderstood.

The office did not hire anyone. Instead, it posted a one-page guide at the entrance and on its website, with pictures that answered those three questions step by step. For the first week, a staff member stood at the door and pointed students to the guide before they joined the line.

By October the average wait at Window 3 had dropped from sixty-five minutes to twenty. The counselors had the same number of hours in the day, but they were finally spending them on the problems only a counselor could solve. The line had never been a staffing problem. It had been a question problem, hiding in plain sight.

  1. What is the main idea of Passage H?
    A. The financial aid office needed more workers.
    B. Finding out why students were waiting solved a problem that hiring could not.
    C. Students should read a website before they visit an office.
    D. Ms. Tran was the best counselor at the college.
  2. Which detail best supports the main idea?
    A. Students waited at Window 3.
    B. The problem happened every September.
    C. Without hiring anyone, the office cut the average wait from sixty-five minutes to twenty.
    D. A staff member stood at the door for one week.
  3. Why did Ms. Tran write down the reason for every visit?
    A. She wanted to find out what was really causing the long line.
    B. Her supervisor asked for a report on staff hours.
    C. She was training new workers.
    D. The college required it every September.
  4. In this passage, the word pattern most nearly means
    A. a design printed on cloth
    B. a set of directions for sewing
    C. a person that others copy
    D. something that happens again and again in the same way
  5. The prefix mis– in misunderstood tells you that most students understood the term
    A. again
    B. wrongly
    C. early
    D. completely
  6. The writer says the cause was "hiding in plain sight." This phrase means the cause was
    A. kept secret by the staff
    B. printed on the website
    C. easy to see, but no one had looked for it
    D. too hard for anyone to find
  7. Which conclusion is best supported by the passage?
    A. Before adding workers or money, it helps to find out what people actually need.
    B. Hiring more staff never helps a busy office.
    C. Most students do not like to wait in line.
    D. Websites work better than counselors.
  8. The writer most likely includes the sentence "The counselors had the same number of hours in the day, but they were finally spending them on the problems only a counselor could solve" to show that
    A. the counselors were working longer shifts
    B. the counselors wanted to see fewer students
    C. the office's budget grew in October
    D. the guide freed the counselors to spend their time on work that needed them
Answer key — open only after time is called
  1. B. A is what the staff assumed, not what the passage proves. The whole passage shows that finding the real questions fixed the line.
  2. C. It is the only choice that shows the result: a shorter wait with no new workers.
  3. A. "Decided to look more closely" tells you she was searching for the real cause.
  4. D. Ms. Tran saw the same three questions again and again. Pattern has other meanings, but only D fits this sentence.
  5. B. Mis– means wrongly or badly, as in misspell and mistake.
  6. C. This is an idiom. The cause was in front of everyone the whole time, but no one had looked for it.
  7. A. B, C and D go further than the passage does. A is what the office actually learned.
  8. D. The guide answered the simple questions, so the counselors had time for the hard ones.
